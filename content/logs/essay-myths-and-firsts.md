First-pass verification from the October 2026 accuracy review (docs/research/accuracy-review-2026-10.md), then claim-by-claim against the sources listed.

- **e1** established · secondary-summary (key scholarship read only via summaries/abstracts): Historians, notably Katharine Park, have shown that the medieval Church did not prohibit human dissection, and that dissections and autopsies were carried out in Italian cities from around 1300.
- **e2** established · secondary-summary (key scholarship read only via summaries/abstracts): Pope Boniface VIII's bull Detestande feritatis (1299/1300) forbade boiling and dismembering corpses to carry the bones home for burial; it was later misread as a ban on anatomical dissection.
- **e3** established: Isaac Vossius, in a book published in London in 1685, was among the first Europeans to claim that the Chinese had known the circulation of the blood for thousands of years, citing texts including the Huangdi Neijing.
- **e4** contested · secondary-summary (key scholarship read only via summaries/abstracts): Joseph Needham's translations suggested the classics described a circulation of vital energy with the heart as a pump; Paul Unschuld translates the same passages as describing neither blood circulation nor a pumping heart.
- **e5** established: In drawings of about 1512–13, Leonardo proposed that eddies forming in the sinuses behind the aortic valve cusps help close the valve, a mechanism later confirmed by modern studies including MRI; his anatomical notes went unpublished for centuries.
- **e6** established: Michael Servetus described the pulmonary circulation in a suppressed theological book of 1553; Valverde put it in an anatomy book in 1556, crediting Colombo, whose own book appeared in 1559.
- **e7** established: In August 1677 the medical student Johan Ham showed Antonie van Leeuwenhoek moving 'animalcules' in semen; Leeuwenhoek reported them to the Royal Society that November and recorded that Ham had seen them first.
- **e8** established: Paolo Mascagni described lymphatics in the cerebral dura mater in his 1787 atlas; two independent groups reported meningeal lymphatic vessels again in 2015.
- **e9** contested: In 2018 Benias, Theise and colleagues reported fluid-filled spaces in connective tissue throughout the body; press coverage called it a 'new organ', a label other anatomists disputed.

First-pass verification from the October 2026 accuracy review (docs/research/accuracy-review-2026-10.md), then claim-by-claim against the sources listed.

- **e1** established: In Galenic physiology blood was made continuously in the liver from digested food, carried outwards by the veins and consumed by the body.
- **e2** established: Aselli saw the lacteals in 1622 while vivisecting a dog that had been fed shortly before; his account was published after his death, in Milan in 1627, with colour-printed woodcuts.
- **e3** established: Aselli believed the lacteals carried chyle to the liver.
- **e4** established: Jean Pecquet's discovery of the thoracic duct, published in his Experimenta nova anatomica (1651), showed that the lacteals do not lead to the liver.
- **e5** contested: In 1653 Olof Rudbeck in Sweden and Thomas Bartholin in Copenhagen each published an account of lymphatic vessels beyond the gut, Rudbeck's Nova exercitatio anatomica and Bartholin's Vasa lymphatica, and a long dispute over priority followed.
- **e6** established: Paolo Mascagni's atlas of the lymphatic system, published in 1787, described lymphatics in the cerebral dura mater; it was written in Latin and largely overlooked.
- **e7** contested: Later nineteenth-century work, notably by Key and Retzius, influenced the long-standing view that the brain lacks lymphatic drainage.
- **e8** established: In 2012 Iliff, Nedergaard and colleagues described in mice a paravascular pathway by which cerebrospinal fluid moves through brain tissue and clears solutes, including amyloid beta, and called it the glymphatic system.
- **e9** contested: How the glymphatic pathway works, and how important it is in humans, remain debated.
- **e10** established: In 2015 two independent groups, Louveau and colleagues in Nature and Aspelund and colleagues in the Journal of Experimental Medicine, reported lymphatic vessels in the meninges.

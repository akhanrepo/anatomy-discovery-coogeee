First-pass verification from the October 2026 accuracy review (docs/research/accuracy-review-2026-10.md), then claim-by-claim against the sources listed.

- **e1** established · secondary-summary (key scholarship read only via summaries/abstracts): Galen showed, by tying off a stretch of artery in a living animal, that arteries naturally contain blood rather than pneuma.
- **e2** established: In Galenic physiology blood was made continuously in the liver from digested food, carried outwards by the veins and consumed by the body.
- **e3** established: Galen taught that blood passes from the right to the left side of the heart through pores in the septum, and Ibn Sina's Canon repeated the teaching.
- **e4** contested: The Canon was translated into Latin in the twelfth century, traditionally credited to Gerard of Cremona, and became a standard textbook in European medical schools for centuries.
- **e5** established: Ibn al-Nafis rejected the septal pores and described blood passing from the right ventricle through the lungs to the left; his commentary was rediscovered in Berlin in 1924.
- **e6** contested: In Europe the lung route was described by Servetus in a suppressed theological book of 1553, by Valverde in print in 1556, and by Colombo in 1559.
- **e7** established: Fabricius described the valves of the veins in 1603 but believed they slowed blood flowing away from the heart.
- **e8** established: Harvey's De motu cordis (1628) argued from the quantity of blood the heart moves that the blood must circulate, and showed that the venous valves let blood flow only towards the heart.
- **e9** established: Harvey inferred but never saw the connection between arteries and veins; in 1661 Malpighi, with Carlo Fracassati, observed blood in minute vessels of the frog lung.
- **e10** established: William Harvey studied at Padua, where Fabricius taught, and later reinterpreted his teacher's venous valves.

First-pass verification from the October 2026 accuracy review (docs/research/accuracy-review-2026-10.md), then claim-by-claim against the sources listed.

- **e1** established: The Edwin Smith Papyrus, a copy made around 1700–1600 BCE from older material, contains the earliest known written descriptions of the brain's surface, the meninges and the cerebrospinal fluid, and the earliest written word for 'brain'.
- **e2** contested: Alcmaeon of Croton held the brain to be the seat of understanding; the report that he dissected an eye comes from a much later source and is doubted.
- **e3** established: In Alexandria Herophilus distinguished nerves from blood vessels and tendons, separated sensory from motor nerves and described several pairs of cranial nerves.
- **e4** contested: The accusation that Herophilus and Erasistratus vivisected condemned criminals rests on Celsus, writing about 250 years later, and on Tertullian, and is doubted by many modern scholars.
- **e5** established: Galen demonstrated before an audience in Rome that cutting the recurrent laryngeal nerves silenced a squealing pig, and Vesalius later repeated the demonstration.
- **e6** established · secondary-summary (key scholarship read only via summaries/abstracts): Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics (1011–1021) argued that vision happens when light enters the eye; his eye anatomy itself was largely Galenic.
- **e7** established: Galen described a rete mirabile at the base of the brain from animal anatomy; Vesalius denied it existed in humans, though it lingered in some anatomical writing after 1543.
- **e8** established: Thomas Willis's Cerebri anatome (1664), illustrated mostly by Christopher Wren, gave the first complete description of the arterial circle at the base of the brain and assigned it a function.
- **e9** established: Using a modified version of Golgi's silver stain, Cajal showed in 1888–89 that nerve cells are separate units; the neuron doctrine was named by Waldeyer in 1891, and Golgi, who shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with Cajal, still defended the reticular theory in his Nobel lecture.
- **e10** established: Paolo Mascagni described lymphatic vessels in the dura mater in 1787; the finding was largely forgotten until two 2015 studies reported meningeal lymphatics.
- **e11** established: The papyrus's cases give each injury a prognosis, and some are judged untreatable.

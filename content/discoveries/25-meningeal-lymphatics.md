In 2015 the news was everywhere: scientists had found lymphatic vessels in the coverings of the brain, something textbooks said did not exist. Two groups reported them independently, one in *Nature* and one in the *Journal of Experimental Medicine*.[^c1] Lymphatic vessels drain fluid and carry immune cells. If the brain has them, a lot of thinking about brain disease and the immune system would have to change.

It was a real advance, made with modern imaging and labelling that no earlier anatomist had. But it wasn't the first description. In 1787 the Italian anatomist Paolo Mascagni published a great atlas of the lymphatic system, and in it he described lymphatics in the dura mater, the tough outer covering of the brain.[^c2] It was in Latin, and it was largely forgotten.

Later anatomy turned the other way. Nineteenth-century work, including that of Key and Retzius, fed a consensus that the brain has no lymphatics, and that view held for a long time.[^c3] So this plate is marked *contested* on purpose. The vessels ink in at 2015, the date modern anatomy accepted them, and the story credits the man who saw them first.

Gabriele Falloppio taught anatomy at Padua, where Vesalius had taught, and spent a book politely correcting him. *Observationes anatomicae*, printed in Venice in 1561, was the only book he published in his lifetime: a running commentary on the *Fabrica* that fixed errors and filled gaps.[^c1]

One correction is still in our vocabulary. Following Galen, anatomists had tended to describe female anatomy as an inverted copy of male anatomy. Vesalius treated the slender tube beside the uterus as a vessel carrying seed, a counterpart to male structures. Falloppio looked again. The tube, he wrote, is its own structure. It flares at its end like a trumpet, and he named it the *uteri tuba*, the trumpet of the uterus.[^c2] Anatomists have called it the Fallopian tube ever since.

What it actually did, carrying eggs from the ovary, would take later centuries to establish. So on this plate the tube inks in as an outline: described, not yet understood. It appears on the female figure; switch figures to see it.

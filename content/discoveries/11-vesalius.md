In 1543 a young professor at Padua published a book so large and so beautiful that it changed what an anatomy book could be. *De humani corporis fabrica*, *On the Fabric of the Human Body*, was printed in Basel with woodcuts of astonishing precision.[^c1]

Vesalius did something his teachers rarely did: he cut the bodies himself. And as he did, he kept finding that Galen was wrong about humans. Galen, unable to dissect people, had learned largely from apes, pigs and dogs. In an animal like the dog the lower jaw is two bones joined at the chin, and Galen said the same of humans. Vesalius showed the human mandible is one bone.[^c2]

The most famous casualty was the *rete mirabile*, a "wonderful net" of vessels that Galen placed under the brain, where it supposedly refined the spirits. It exists in sheep and oxen. Vesalius could not find it in people, and said so.[^c3] It didn't vanish overnight, though. The net lingered in some textbooks for years.

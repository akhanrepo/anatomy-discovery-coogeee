In March 2018 headlines announced that scientists had found a new organ, perhaps the largest in the body. The paper behind them, by Petros Benias, Neil Theise and colleagues, was more careful.

Using a probe that images living tissue during endoscopy, they saw layers of connective tissue under the skin, around the gut and in other organs, that were full of fluid-filled spaces held open by a lattice of fibres. On ordinary microscope slides these layers look dense and solid. Their explanation was that preparing tissue for slides drains the fluid, and the spaces collapse.[^c1]

Was it a new organ? Other anatomists praised the imaging but called the "new organ" label a distraction, and later commentary pointed out that the interstitium itself was hardly new. What was new was the view.[^c2]

That argument is exactly what this site is about. Anatomy isn't finished. "Discovery" is often a better way of seeing something that was already there, and the labels we give it are part of the history too. This is the last plate, and it is marked *contested*.

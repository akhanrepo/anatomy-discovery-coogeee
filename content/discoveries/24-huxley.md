Muscles had been described since antiquity; Suśruta's students peeled them back layer by layer. But how does a muscle actually shorten? For much of the twentieth century a popular answer was that long protein molecules fold or coil, like a spring.

On 22 May 1954, *Nature* printed two papers side by side.[^c1] In one, the physiologist Andrew Huxley and his colleague Rolf Niedergerke had used a new interference microscope on living frog muscle fibres. In the other, Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson had studied isolated myofibrils. With different methods and different materials, they reached the same conclusion. The filaments in muscle don't shorten at all. Two sets of them slide past each other, like interlacing fingers.[^c2]

And no, the two Huxleys were not related.[^c3]

This is the first plate where understanding reaches down to molecules. Skeletal muscle, outlined since antiquity, fills with colour here. It is also one of our rare discoveries with a verified day and month, so it appears as an "On this day" card every 22 May.

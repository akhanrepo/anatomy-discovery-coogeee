Harvey's circulation had a hole in the middle. Blood left the heart in arteries and came back in veins, but how did it cross from one to the other? Harvey supposed it filtered through the tissues somehow. He never saw it happen.

Marcello Malpighi, in Bologna, had something Harvey lacked: a good microscope. In two letters to his friend Giovanni Borelli, printed in 1661, he first showed that the lung is not a lump of flesh but a mass of tiny membranous air sacs.[^c1]

Then he turned to the frog, whose lungs are thin enough to see through. With his colleague Carlo Fracassati he watched blood moving through vessels too fine for the naked eye, carrying it from the smallest arteries into the smallest veins.[^c2] The circuit was closed. The vessels would later be called capillaries, after the Latin for "hair-like".

Under our rules this is *microscopic* observation: the capillaries ink in as an outline, seen at last. Their full role in exchanging oxygen and nutrients would take another two centuries to work out.

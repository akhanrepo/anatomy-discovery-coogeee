For a thousand years, physicians learned that blood crosses the heart from right to left through tiny invisible pores in its central wall, the septum. Galen taught it, and Ibn Sina's *Canon*, the greatest medical textbook of the Islamic world, repeated it.

Ibn al-Nafis, a physician trained in Damascus who worked in Cairo, disagreed. In his commentary on the anatomy of the *Canon*, he wrote that the septum is solid and has no visible or invisible passage. Blood from the right side of the heart must therefore go out through the pulmonary artery, pass through the lungs, mix with air, and return through the pulmonary vein to the left side.[^c1] That is the pulmonary circulation, about three centuries before anyone in Europe described it.

Then the idea vanished from view. The manuscript lay unread in a Berlin library until 1924, when an Egyptian physician, Muhyo al-Deen Altatawi, recognised what it said.[^c2] Did it travel west earlier, to inspire the Europeans of the 1550s? Possibly. Historians still argue about it.[^c3]

Watch the red pores in the septum: on this plate they begin to fade.

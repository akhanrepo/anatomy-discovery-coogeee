Around 500 BCE, in a Greek colony in southern Italy, a physician-philosopher named Alcmaeon made a claim that sounds obvious now and was not obvious then: we understand with the brain.[^c1] Many of his contemporaries placed thought and feeling in the chest.

How did he get there? Later writers say he reasoned from the senses. The eyes, ears and nose all sit in the head, and Alcmaeon spoke of channels, *poroi*, running from them to the brain.[^c2] He also drew a distinction philosophers still use, between perceiving something and understanding it.

The famous part of his story is the shakiest. A writer of the fourth century CE says Alcmaeon was the first to dare to cut, and that he excised an eye. That would make him the discoverer of the optic nerve. But the testimony is very late, and most careful scholars doubt he dissected as a regular practice.[^c3]

So this plate carries a *contested* mark. The idea is secure. The scalpel may be a legend added later, the kind of founding myth that the history of anatomy collects.

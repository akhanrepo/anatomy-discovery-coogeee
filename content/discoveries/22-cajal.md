In the 1880s many leading scientists believed the nervous system was one continuous web. Nerve fibres fused into a net, and signals flowed through it like water through pipes. The Italian Camillo Golgi was its greatest champion. He had also invented the best tool for testing it: a silver stain that turns a few nerve cells, seemingly at random, completely black, so that each one stands out whole.

A Spanish anatomist, Santiago Ramón y Cajal, improved Golgi's stain and used it on the young brains and retinas of birds, where cells are less tangled. In 1888 and 1889 he found that the fibres don't fuse. Each nerve cell is a separate unit, its branches ending close to the next cell without joining it.[^c1] He drew what he saw in thousands of exquisite drawings.

In 1906 the Nobel committee gave its prize to both men. In his lecture, Golgi still defended the net.[^c2] The idea got its name, the neuron doctrine, from Wilhelm Waldeyer in 1891, and electron microscopy later confirmed that each neuron is sealed in its own membrane.[^c3]

Under our rules these are microscopic observations, so the brain's cellular structure inks in as a new layer of description.

For a few decades around 300 BCE, Alexandria allowed something almost no other ancient city did: the dissection of human bodies. Two Greek physicians made the most of it.

Herophilus traced the white cords of the body and saw that they were not all the same thing. Some were tendons, some were vessels, and some were nerves, connected to the brain and spinal cord. He separated nerves that carry sensation from nerves that move muscles, and described several pairs of cranial nerves.[^c1] Erasistratus, a younger contemporary, worked in the same city and is remembered alongside him as a founder of anatomy based on the human body.

Their reputation carries a darker story. Celsus, writing about 250 years later, says they cut open living criminals supplied by the king. A Christian writer, Tertullian, called Herophilus a butcher. But Galen, who criticised both men freely, never mentions vivisection, and many historians now treat the charge as hearsay.[^c2]

Then the window closed. Human dissection faded, and the next great anatomist, Galen, would learn mostly from animals.

In July 1622 Gaspare Aselli was vivisecting a dog in front of colleagues. The animal had eaten shortly before. When he opened its abdomen he saw something nobody had described properly: fine white threads running through the membrane that holds the gut, like veins filled with milk.[^c1]

These were vessels carrying chyle, the fat-rich product of digestion, and they were part of a system no one knew existed. He called them *venae lacteae*, the milky veins.

Aselli did not live to publish. His book, *De lactibus sive lacteis venis*, came out in Milan in 1627, after his death. Its woodcuts are among the first colour-printed illustrations in an anatomy book.[^c2]

He made one large mistake. Galen's system said digested food goes to the liver to be made into blood, so Aselli assumed his milky veins led there too. It took Jean Pecquet, around 1650–51, to find the thoracic duct and show they lead elsewhere.[^c3] The lacteals ink in as outlines here, and a red line runs from them to the liver.

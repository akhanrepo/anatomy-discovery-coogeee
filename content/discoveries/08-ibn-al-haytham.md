How do we see? For centuries, many scholars thought the eye sends out rays that touch the world, like fingers. It explained a lot, and it had the authority of Euclid and Ptolemy behind it.

Between about 1011 and 1021, Ibn al-Haytham, a mathematician working in Cairo, wrote the *Kitāb al-Manāẓir*, the Book of Optics.[^c1] He took the older theories apart one by one, and argued for the reverse: light from every point of an object travels in straight lines, and some of it enters the eye. Vision happens when light comes in, not when rays go out.[^c2] His arguments rested on observation and experiment, and on geometry he made rigorous.

It's worth being exact about what this plate claims. Ibn al-Haytham didn't discover new parts of the eye; his anatomy came largely from Galen.[^c3] What changed was the eye's *function*: what it does and how. That's why the eye inks in fully here.

Translated into Latin as *De aspectibus*, the book shaped European optics for centuries, all the way to Kepler.

Under the brain, the arteries that supply it join up in a ring. Block one feeding vessel and blood can often still reach the brain the long way round.

Earlier anatomists had seen and sketched parts of this ring. Thomas Willis, an Oxford physician, was the first to describe it completely, and the first to say what it was for.[^c2] His *Cerebri anatome* of 1664 went far beyond the circle. It mapped the brain and its nerves in new detail, and it was illustrated by a young astronomy professor who would later rebuild London's churches: Christopher Wren.[^c1]

Willis worked with a remarkable Oxford circle of experimenters, and the book reflects that collaboration: it is anatomy written by a practising physician, with an eye on what goes wrong in patients.

He didn't name the ring after himself. The phrase "circle of Willis" was attached later.[^c3] On this plate the circle inks in fully, both its structure and its function.

Galen loved an audience, and this was his showpiece. A pig squeals; that is what pigs do when restrained. Galen exposed a pair of slender nerves in its neck. They come down from the brain, loop back up and reach the larynx. He cut them, and the squealing stopped. The pig still struggled and breathed, but it had no voice.[^c1]

The point was polemical. Influential thinkers held that the heart rules the body, and some placed the voice there. Galen showed, with an animal and a blade, that the voice depended on nerves running from the brain.

The demonstration became a classic. Fourteen centuries later Andreas Vesalius repeated it, tying the nerves and then loosening them so the squeal returned.[^c2] Retellings don't always agree on details, because Galen ran several experiments on the voice, and one modern account describes him tying the intercostal nerves instead.[^c3]

Thyroid surgeons still learn this nerve's name early, because cutting it by accident does to a patient what Galen did on purpose.

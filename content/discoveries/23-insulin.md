By 1920 physiologists suspected that the islets Langerhans had seen produced something that controls blood sugar. Nobody had managed to extract it in a form that worked.

In 1921 a young surgeon, Frederick Banting, persuaded the physiologist J. J. R. Macleod at the University of Toronto to give him laboratory space, dogs and a student assistant, Charles Best. Their extracts lowered blood sugar in diabetic dogs. The biochemist James Collip then purified the extract well enough to treat people.[^c1] Within months, patients who would have died were alive.

The credit was much harder to share. The 1923 Nobel Prize went to Banting and Macleod. Banting, furious that Best had been left out, gave Best half his prize money. Macleod gave half of his to Collip.[^c2] The participants' grievances outlived them all, which is why this plate is marked *contested*: not the science, the credit.

Drag to 1921 and the pancreas, outlined since 1869, fills with colour. Half a century after it was seen, it is understood.

Galen of Pergamon was a gladiators' doctor, a court physician and the most prolific medical writer of antiquity. He was also a superb experimenter, and this is one of his cleanest experiments.

The followers of Erasistratus taught that arteries carry a subtle, air-like *pneuma*. If you cut an artery and blood spurts out, they said, that is because blood rushes in from the veins to fill the gap left by the escaping *pneuma*. It was a clever answer, and hard to refute by argument alone.

So Galen stopped arguing. In a treatise devoted to the question, he describes exposing an artery in a living animal and tying it off at two points.[^c1] Nothing could rush into the stretch between the ties. When he opened it, it was full of blood.[^c2]

The arteries ink in fully on this plate: their contents, at least, were understood. But Galen's larger system, in which blood is made in the liver and seeps through invisible pores in the heart, would stand for more than a thousand years. You can see those beliefs in red ink.

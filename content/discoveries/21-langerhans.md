In 1869 a 22-year-old medical student in Berlin, Paul Langerhans, was looking at thin slices of pancreas under a microscope for his doctoral dissertation. Most of the gland was what everyone expected: cells that make digestive juices. But scattered among them were small, distinct clusters of different-looking cells.[^c1]

Langerhans described them carefully, and admitted he did not know what they were for. That honesty is exactly the point of this plate. A structure can be seen, drawn and named long before anyone understands it. The clusters were later named the islets of Langerhans in his honour, and only in the twentieth century were they tied to insulin and to the control of blood sugar.[^c2]

Most of the body's systems announce themselves. Bones are hard and white, the heart beats, nerves are firm cords, the gut is unmistakable. The lymphatic system does not. Its vessels are thin-walled and usually carry clear fluid, and in a body that has been dead for some time they collapse and are easily missed. It is no accident that the system was found late, found by chance, and then found again.

What the system does is easy to state in modern terms. Fluid constantly leaks out of the smallest blood vessels into the tissues. Lymphatic vessels collect it and return it to the blood, filtering it through lymph nodes on the way, where cells of the immune system meet whatever it carries. In the gut, the same vessels pick up absorbed fat. None of this was known when the story begins, and almost every part of it had to be learned the hard way: by noticing vessels that did not fit the existing picture and asking where they went.

This is the shortest tour on the site, with only two plates. They sit almost four centuries apart, and the long gap between them is the subject of this chapter.

## The liver at the centre

To see why the first plate mattered, start with the model it disturbed. In Galenic physiology, the liver was the body's great workshop. Food, digested in the stomach and gut, was carried to the liver, made into blood there, and sent out through the veins to be consumed by the tissues.[^e1] It was a coherent system, and it answered a question that anyone could ask: where does the food go?

The weak point in the system was the route from gut to liver. The veins of the gut, which do run to the liver, seemed to be the obvious answer, and the Galenic model gave anatomists no reason to look for another one.

## A dog that had eaten

In July 1622 Gaspare Aselli, a surgeon in Milan, was vivisecting a dog in front of colleagues. The dog had been fed shortly before, and when he opened its abdomen he saw fine white threads in the membrane that holds the gut. They were vessels filled with chyle, the milky, fat-rich fluid of digestion.[^e2] Had the dog been hungry, they would have been almost invisible.

Aselli called them the *venae lacteae*, the milky veins. His book about them was published in 1627, after his death, with woodcuts printed in colour, among the first in an anatomy book.[^e2] It is one of the great accidents in this history. A discovery that depended on the timing of a dog's last meal opened a whole system to view.

But Aselli read what he saw through the model he had. Galen's system said the products of digestion go to the liver, so he assumed his milky veins went there too.[^e3] On this site, a red line runs from the lacteals to the liver from 1622. It is one of the clearest cases on the site of an anatomist seeing something new and fitting it into an old frame.

## Following the milk

The correction came about a generation later. Jean Pecquet, working in France, found that the lacteals do not lead to the liver at all. They drain into a collecting duct that runs up through the chest, the thoracic duct, which empties into the great veins at the base of the neck. He published the finding in 1651.[^e4] On the site, the red line erases that year.

Pecquet's discovery did more damage to the Galenic system than it might seem. If the chyle bypasses the liver and goes straight into the great veins on their way to the heart, then the liver is not the place where food becomes blood. Harvey's *De motu cordis* had already made the heart the centre of the circulation in 1628. Pecquet's duct moved digestion's product to the same place.

## A system, and a quarrel

The lacteals were only one part of a larger network. In 1653 two anatomists published accounts of lymphatic vessels elsewhere in the body: Olof Rudbeck in Sweden, in his *Nova exercitatio anatomica*, and Thomas Bartholin in Copenhagen, in a work titled *Vasa lymphatica*. A long dispute over priority followed.[^e5]

The dispute was bitter, and its details are still argued over; the source note records the disagreement. What is not in doubt is that within about thirty years of Aselli's dog, European anatomists had gone from not knowing the system existed to arguing over who deserved the credit for describing it. That is how fast an anatomy can be filled in once someone knows what to look for.

The priority quarrel also makes a broader point, one that runs through the myths chapter. The question of who saw something first is often less important than the change in what everyone could see afterwards. Once there was a lymphatic system to look for, it was found throughout the body.

## The great atlas

The eighteenth century brought the lymphatic system its great atlas. In 1787 the Italian anatomist Paolo Mascagni published a magnificent illustrated account of the lymphatic vessels of the human body, built on long work in dissection and injection.[^e6] Among much else, he described lymphatic vessels in the dura mater, the tough outer covering of the brain.

That particular observation, written in Latin, was largely overlooked.[^e6]

## How a system was taken away

In the nineteenth century, anatomy moved the other way. Work on the fluid spaces of the brain and spinal cord, notably by Axel Key and Gustaf Retzius, fed a consensus that the brain had no lymphatic drainage of its own.[^e7] How much any single work shaped that consensus is debated. What matters for this chapter is that it held: for more than a century, textbooks taught that the central nervous system was the one part of the body without lymphatics.

On the site, this appears as a misbelief. The red ink around the brain's coverings appears in 1875 and stays until 2015. It is unusual among the site's misbeliefs, because it was not an ancient error handed down from Galen. It was a modern one, held by careful anatomists using good microscopes. It is a reminder that the fog on this site does not only clear. Sometimes a correct description is lost, and something wrong takes its place.

## A new route for fluid

The question came back through a different door. In 2012 Jeffrey Iliff, Maiken Nedergaard and colleagues described, in mice, a pathway by which the fluid around the brain flows along the outsides of blood vessels and through brain tissue, clearing waste including amyloid beta, the protein that builds up in Alzheimer's disease. The pathway became known as the glymphatic system, because it seemed to do the work of lymphatics in an organ thought to have none.[^e8]

The idea drew a great deal of attention, and also a great deal of argument. How fluid actually moves through brain tissue, and how important the pathway is in people rather than mice, are still debated.[^e9] It does not have a plate on this site, because it has not yet settled into the kind of agreement that the fog engine records as knowledge. But it changed the question people were asking. If the brain needs a way to clear fluid and waste, where does the fluid go?

## Found again

In 2015 two groups, working independently, answered part of that question. Antoine Louveau, Jonathan Kipnis and colleagues in Virginia, publishing in *Nature*, and Aleksanteri Aspelund, Kari Alitalo and colleagues in Helsinki, publishing in the *Journal of Experimental Medicine*, reported lymphatic vessels in the meninges, the coverings of the brain.[^e10] The vessels were thin, ran alongside large blood vessels in the dura, and had been missed partly because, like all lymphatics, they are hard to see in tissue prepared the usual way.

The stakes were high. Lymphatic vessels are one of the main routes by which the immune system watches a tissue. An organ with no lymphatics would be cut off from that traffic, which was one of the reasons the brain had long been thought of as a special case. If the brain's coverings drain into the lymph nodes of the neck like other tissues, then a good deal of thinking about inflammation, infection and neurodegenerative disease in the brain had to be revisited.

The news spread as a discovery, and in an important sense it was one. The modern work used imaging and labelling that no earlier anatomist had, and it showed the vessels at work in living animals. But the vessels had been drawn before, by Mascagni, two centuries earlier.[^e6] That is why the plate is marked *contested*, and why its story credits the man who saw them first.

## Pictures as evidence

The lymphatic system's history is also a history of pictures. Aselli's book used colour, printed from woodcuts, to show white vessels against the dark tissue of the gut, at a time when almost every anatomical illustration was black on white.[^e2] Mascagni's case rested on his plates.[^e6] And the modern rediscovery rested on images too: fluorescent labelling and microscopy that made thin, clear vessels stand out against everything around them.[^e10]

This is not a coincidence. A structure that is pale, thin and easily collapsed has to be *shown* before it can be believed, and the means of showing it shape what can be claimed. A description in words that the vessels exist is easy to doubt. A picture that others can compare with their own dissections is harder to dismiss, and a method others can repeat is harder still. Each of the turning points in this chapter came when someone found a better way to make the vessels visible to other people, not only to themselves.

It also explains why Mascagni's observation could be lost. A plate in a large and expensive Latin atlas, read by a few specialists, is evidence only for as long as people keep looking at it. Once the consensus had turned, there was no reason to go back to the page.

## Reading the two plates

On the site, the two plates in this chapter behave differently, and the difference is deliberate.

The lacteals ink in as an outline in 1622. They are drawn as *described*, not *understood*, because Aselli saw them and got their purpose wrong. The red line from the lacteals to the liver, which marks his mistake, appears in the same year and is erased in 1651, when Pecquet showed where they really lead.[^e4]

The meningeal lymphatics ink in only in 2015. Before that, the brain's coverings carry the red mark of a misbelief from 1875, the date this site uses for the consensus that the brain has no lymphatics.[^e7] Mascagni's earlier description does not ink the vessels in, because the site draws knowledge by when it became accepted rather than by when it first appeared on a page. His name is on the plate, and his date is in its title, so the forgotten first is credited without pretending it was known.

That rule is the hardest judgement in the whole fog engine, and this chapter is where it is most visible. Readers who disagree with it are invited to read the plate's note and the sources, which set out the case either way.

## Two kinds of hidden

The two plates in this chapter show two different ways a structure can be hidden.

The lacteals were hidden **physically**. They are only easy to see in an animal that has just eaten, at just the right moment, and nobody had looked at the right time. Once Aselli had seen them, the problem was solved quickly. Within a generation, Pecquet had traced where they went, and Rudbeck and Bartholin had found their relatives throughout the body.

The meningeal lymphatics were hidden **by knowledge**. They had been described. But a later consensus said they could not exist, and a structure that cannot exist is very hard to see. Each generation of students learned the consensus, so nobody looked for them, and nobody looked at Mascagni's plate with fresh eyes.

The first kind of hiding is the kind that better instruments cure. The second kind is harder, because it is made of things everyone is sure of. It is the reason this site insists on showing what people believed at each moment, and not only what was true. If you drag the slider through this chapter, you will see the brain's coverings marked in red from 1875, and the vessels themselves ink in only in 2015, although Mascagni had drawn them in 1787. That path, rather than a straight line, is what knowledge usually looks like.

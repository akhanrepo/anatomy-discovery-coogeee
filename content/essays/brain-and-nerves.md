Where does thinking happen? For most of history that was not an anatomical question at all. It was a question about the soul, about the heart and the breath, about what makes a person a person. This chapter follows how it became a question about tissue, and then about cells, and how much of the answer depended on how people were allowed to look.

## A wound, not a theory

The brain enters written history in an injury report. An Egyptian surgical manual, known from a copy made around 1700–1600 BCE from older material, describes what a surgeon saw under a broken skull: the folded surface of the brain, a membrane over it, and fluid. These are the earliest known written descriptions of the meninges and the cerebrospinal fluid, and the text contains the earliest written word for "brain".[^e1]

What the papyrus does not contain is a theory of what the brain is for. Its author noticed that head wounds could affect the body, and recorded what happened, case by case. That is the right place for this history to begin: with observation running ahead of explanation.

The manual is also honest in a way later texts often were not. Each case ends with a verdict, and some injuries are judged beyond help.[^e11] A surgeon who writes down which wounds he cannot treat is already thinking about evidence. He is separating what he has seen from what he hopes. Much of the rest of this chapter is about that same separation, made again and again under different conditions: in Greek philosophy, in Alexandrian dissection rooms, in Roman demonstrations, in Oxford and in the microscope rooms of Barcelona.

## The head against the heart

In the Greek world the explanation came first. Around 500 BCE Alcmaeon of Croton argued that we understand with the brain, and spoke of channels connecting the senses to it. A later writer says he dissected an eye; most careful scholars doubt it.[^e2] The idea had a long fight ahead. Many thinkers, before and after, placed thought and feeling in the chest.

The argument shifted when anatomists could cut. For a few decades around 300 BCE, Alexandria permitted the dissection of human bodies, and Herophilus used the opportunity. He separated nerves from the tendons and vessels they resemble, distinguished nerves that carry sensation from those that move muscles, and described several pairs of cranial nerves.[^e3] The same men were later accused of cutting open living criminals, but the charge comes from a writer who lived about 250 years after them and from a hostile Christian polemicist, and many historians now doubt it.[^e4]

The Alexandrian window closed. When the argument resumed, it resumed with animals.

## A pig falls silent

Galen, working in second-century Rome, could not dissect people, but he could stage experiments. His most famous was a public one: he exposed the nerves that run down the neck and loop back up to the larynx, cut them, and a squealing pig fell silent.[^e5] The demonstration argued, against those who placed the ruling part of the body in the heart, that the voice depended on nerves from the brain. Fourteen centuries later Vesalius repeated it.[^e5]

Galen's reliance on animals had a cost, and the brain shows it clearly. Under the brains of animals such as sheep and oxen lies a net of vessels, the *rete mirabile*. Galen placed it in humans too, and gave it a role in refining the spirits that animated the body. When Vesalius dissected human brains in the sixteenth century, he could not find it, and said so; even then, the net lingered in some anatomical writing.[^e7]

## Seeing is a function too

Not every step in this chapter was a dissection. Between about 1011 and 1021 Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, wrote the *Book of Optics*. He argued that vision happens when light from every point of an object enters the eye, not when the eye sends out rays. His anatomy of the eye was largely inherited from Galen; what he changed was the understanding of what the eye does.[^e6] The plate for this discovery inks the eye in full for exactly that reason: function, not new structure.

## A ring of arteries and an architect

By the seventeenth century, anatomy was something physicians did to answer questions about disease. Thomas Willis, an Oxford physician, published *Cerebri anatome* in 1664. It mapped the brain and its nerves in new detail and gave the first complete description of the ring of arteries at the base of the brain, explaining what it was for. Its finest illustrations were drawn by Christopher Wren.[^e8] The ring carries Willis's name, although others had glimpsed parts of it and the name was attached later.

## Net or cells?

The last great argument in this chapter happened below the limit of the naked eye. In the late nineteenth century many scientists believed the nervous system was a single continuous net, its fibres fused together. Camillo Golgi, its leading defender, had also invented the stain that would test it: a silver method that blackens a few nerve cells completely, seemingly at random.

Santiago Ramón y Cajal improved the stain and used it on the young brains and retinas of birds. In 1888 and 1889 he showed that the fibres do not fuse; each nerve cell is a separate unit. The idea was named the neuron doctrine by Wilhelm Waldeyer in 1891. In 1906 Golgi and Cajal shared the Nobel Prize, and Golgi used his lecture to defend the net.[^e9]

There is something admirable in that scene, even if Golgi was wrong. He had made the tool, watched a rival use it against him, and still argued his case in public. This history is full of people who were wrong for good reasons.

## Still being drawn

It would be comforting to end with the neuron doctrine and a finished map. The plates do not allow it. In 1787 Paolo Mascagni described lymphatic vessels in the dura mater, the brain's outer covering. The finding was largely forgotten, and textbooks taught that the brain had no lymphatic drainage, until two studies in 2015 reported lymphatic vessels in the meninges.[^e10]

## How the looking changed

Step back from the individual plates and a pattern in the methods appears. The Egyptian surgeon looked at wounds. Alcmaeon reasoned from where the sense organs sit. Herophilus could cut human bodies for a few decades, and learned more about nerves in that time than anyone would for centuries afterwards. Galen experimented on animals, and his greatest errors about the human brain were errors about animals. Ibn al-Haytham changed the question from what the eye is made of to what it does. Willis read anatomy alongside his patients. Cajal needed a chemical stain and a microscope to see that the network was made of separate cells.

Each change in method changed what could be known. That is why the plates in this chapter are dated by when something was described or understood, not by when it was first imagined. Imagining that the head is the seat of thought is very old. Knowing what the head contains, and how its parts connect, took the whole of recorded history.

That is the shape of this chapter: an observation, a long argument about what it means, a correction made with a better way of looking, and then the discovery that something was seen once before. The brain was the last part of the body to be handed over to anatomy, and it is still the part where the drawing is least complete.

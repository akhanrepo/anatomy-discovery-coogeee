This chapter collects the plates where the familiar story is wrong, or contested, or simply less interesting than what happened. Read together, they show that most myths in the history of anatomy are not random errors. They follow a small number of patterns, and once you know the patterns you start to see them everywhere.

## The myth this site had to drop

Start with a myth that is not on any plate, because it is about the conditions for all of them. Many people learn that the medieval Church banned human dissection, and that anatomy could only advance once that ban was defied. Historians, notably Katharine Park, have shown that this is wrong: dissections and autopsies were carried out in Italian cities from around 1300.[^e1]

The myth has a specific origin. In 1299 or 1300 Pope Boniface VIII issued a bull, *Detestande feritatis*, against a funeral custom: boiling and cutting up the bodies of people who died far from home so that their bones could be carried back for burial. Later writers read it as a ban on anatomy.[^e2] It was not. But a story about a heroic science breaking free of a hostile church was too useful to give up, and it is still repeated.

That is the first pattern: **a myth that flatters the teller.** The story makes the moderns look brave and the past look benighted.

## Claimed for the ancients

The second pattern runs the other way. In 1685 Isaac Vossius, in a book published in London, was among the first Europeans to claim that the Chinese had known the circulation of the blood for thousands of years, citing the *Huangdi Neijing*.[^e3] The claim was revived in the twentieth century through Joseph Needham's translations, which read the classics as describing a circulation with the heart as a pump. Paul Unschuld, translating the same passages, finds neither blood circulation nor a pumping heart.[^e4]

This is **a myth of anticipation**: a later idea is read back into an older text that was answering a different question. It is often well-meant, a way of giving a tradition its due. But it does the tradition no favours. The *Huangdi Neijing* has a coherent account of its own, about qi moving in vessels, and treating it as an early draft of Harvey hides what it actually says. On this site the plate is marked *revised*, and it never inks in.

## The genius in the notebook

Leonardo da Vinci is the third pattern. In drawings of about 1512 and 1513 he proposed that eddies forming behind the cusps of the aortic valve help it close after each heartbeat, and modern studies, including MRI, have confirmed that mechanism. But his anatomical notes were not published for centuries.[^e5]

So was Leonardo first? In one sense, plainly yes. In another, his insight played no part in the history of anatomy, because nobody who could have built on it read it. This is **the problem of the unread first**, and it returns throughout this chapter. A discovery that does not reach anyone is still a discovery, but it is not yet knowledge in the sense that matters for a history of what people knew.

## Three claimants and a suppressed book

The pulmonary circulation offers three European candidates within six years. Michael Servetus described it in 1553, in a theological book that was suppressed and almost entirely destroyed. Juan Valverde put it in an anatomy book in 1556 and credited his teacher Realdo Colombo, whose own book appeared in 1559.[^e6] Behind all three stands Ibn al-Nafis, three centuries earlier, whose work was not printed.

Who was first depends on what you count: writing, printing, being read, or persuading other anatomists. That is **the problem of the definition**, and arguments over priority are usually arguments about it in disguise. This site does not settle the question. The plate is marked *contested*, and the story sets out what each man did.

## The student behind the name

In August 1677 a medical student, Johan Ham, showed Antonie van Leeuwenhoek the moving "animalcules" he had seen in semen. Leeuwenhoek reported them to the Royal Society, and he said in that report that Ham had seen them first.[^e7] Popular history still gives the discovery to Leeuwenhoek alone.

This is **the myth of the single name**. Discoveries attach to the person with the reputation, the instrument or the correspondence, and the others drop away. In this case the record is unusually clear, because the famous man credited the student himself. Most such cases leave less evidence.

## Found twice

The last two plates in this chapter are modern, and that is the point. In 2015 two groups reported lymphatic vessels in the coverings of the brain, and the news spread as a discovery. Paolo Mascagni had described lymphatics in the dura mater in 1787.[^e8] In 2018 a team using a new imaging probe reported fluid-filled spaces in connective tissue throughout the body. The press called it a "new organ", and other anatomists disputed the label.[^e9]

Neither story is a fraud, and both involved real advances in method. They belong here because they show the patterns still at work: the forgotten first, and the label that runs ahead of the finding. It would be comforting to think that myths of discovery belong to an earlier, less careful age. They do not. They are made in the present, by press releases and headlines, in the same ways they always were.

## How this site marks them

These patterns are why the plates carry a status as well as a date. A plate marked *established* records something specialists agree on. A plate marked *contested* records a real disagreement among people who have read the evidence, and its note says what the disagreement is. A plate marked *revised* records a claim that was once widely repeated and does not survive a close reading; on the body, it never inks in. Ten of the twenty-six plates carry one of these marks, and the build refuses to publish if fewer than six do. The reason is not to be contrarian. A history that smooths every disagreement away is easier to read, but it is a less accurate record of how knowledge was actually made.

## A short guide for sceptics

When you meet a story about who first discovered something in the body, it is worth asking five questions.

- **Who benefits from this version?** A story that makes one group look heroic and another look foolish deserves a second look.
- **Is a later idea being read into an earlier text?** Check what the text was trying to explain.
- **Was it read?** A discovery that nobody saw did not change what anyone knew.
- **First at what?** Seeing, writing, printing and persuading are different achievements.
- **Who else was in the room?** Students, assistants, collaborators and earlier authors are the first to be forgotten.

None of these questions makes the history less remarkable. They usually make it more so. The real story of the pulmonary circulation, with a Cairo commentator, a burned theologian and a Paduan professor, is better than the legend of a single genius. The real story of the medieval body, opened for autopsies and teaching in Italian cities, is better than the legend of a church that forbade it. And the real story of the meningeal lymphatics, lost and found, is better than a headline about a discovery that came out of nowhere.

The circulation of the blood is usually told as a single flash of insight: in 1628 William Harvey saw what nobody had seen before. The story in this chapter is longer and more interesting than that. It runs for fourteen centuries, it crosses three languages and at least four cities, and almost every step in it was taken by someone who got something important right and something else, just as important, wrong.

## Galen's system

Start with Galen, because everyone after him did. In the second century he settled a real question by experiment. The followers of Erasistratus held that arteries carry an airy *pneuma*, and that blood only appears in a cut artery because it rushes in from elsewhere. Galen tied off a stretch of artery in a living animal, so that nothing could rush in, opened it, and found it full of blood.[^e1] It was a clean result, and it still stands.

But the experiment sat inside a much larger system, and the system was wrong. In Galenic physiology the liver turns digested food into blood, the veins carry that blood outwards to nourish the body, and the body uses it up.[^e2] There is movement in this model, even ebb and flow, but nothing goes round. And the two sides of the heart had to be connected somehow, so Galen taught that blood seeps from the right ventricle to the left through tiny pores in the wall between them, the septum.[^e3]

It is worth pausing on how reasonable all this was. Nobody could see the pores, but nobody could see the capillaries either, and they turned out to be real. A physician who accepted Galen's account was not being lazy or credulous. He was accepting the most complete, most argued, best-evidenced account of the body anyone had produced.

## The book that carried Galen

Galen's ideas did not travel on their own. In the eleventh century Ibn Sina wrote the *Canon of Medicine*, a vast synthesis that organised Greek medicine, including Galen's anatomy, into a teachable whole. The *Canon* repeated the septal pores.[^e3] Translated into Latin in the twelfth century (the translation is traditionally credited to Gerard of Cremona), it became a standard textbook in European medical schools for centuries.[^e4] So when Renaissance students learned about the heart, they were often learning Galen through Ibn Sina, in Latin.

This matters for what happened next, because the first serious attack on the pores came from inside the tradition that had preserved them.

## A commentary that disagreed

Ibn al-Nafis, a physician trained in Damascus who worked in Cairo, wrote a commentary on the anatomy of the *Canon*. Commentaries were a normal way of teaching, and they were expected to explain the master's text. On the septum, Ibn al-Nafis refused. The wall between the ventricles, he wrote, is solid, with no passage visible or invisible. Blood from the right side must therefore leave through the artery to the lungs, pass through the lungs, and return to the left side by the pulmonary vein.[^e5]

This is the pulmonary circulation, described around 1242. It is a remarkable piece of reasoning from structure to function, and it is the reason the red pores on the figure begin to fade at that date. But for centuries almost nobody in Europe knew of it. The manuscript was rediscovered in a Berlin library only in 1924.[^e5] Whether any version of the idea travelled west earlier is one of the open questions of this history, and the plates mark it as contested.

## Three Europeans, one decade

The lung route was described again in sixteenth-century Europe, three times in six years. Michael Servetus, a theologian, put it in a book about the spirit that was published anonymously in 1553 and almost entirely destroyed. Juan Valverde de Hamusco printed it in an anatomy book in 1556 and credited his teacher Realdo Colombo, whose own book followed in 1559.[^e6]

Who was first? It depends on what you count: a theology book that almost nobody could read, a student's textbook, or the master's own work. The more useful observation is that the idea was in the air. Anatomists who dissected for themselves, as Vesalius had urged, were finding that the septum looked solid. Once the pores were in doubt, the lung route was the obvious alternative.

## A brake in the veins

The next clue came from the veins. In 1603 Hieronymus Fabricius, professor at Padua, published the first detailed account of the little valves inside them. He drew them beautifully. He also explained them within Galen's system: the valves, he thought, slowed the blood as it flowed outwards from the heart, so that it would not pool in the hands and feet.[^e7]

The anatomy was right and the function backwards. Fabricius is a useful reminder that careful observation does not, on its own, change a theory. You can see exactly what is there and still fit it into the old picture.

## Harvey's arithmetic

William Harvey studied at Padua, where Fabricius taught.[^e10] His *De motu cordis*, printed in Frankfurt in 1628, asked a question that Galen's system could not survive: how much? Estimate how much blood the heart pushes out with each beat, multiply by the beats in an hour, and the total is far more than the body contains or could make from food. The blood cannot be made and used up at that rate. It must go round. Harvey also showed that the valves Fabricius had drawn let blood flow only one way, towards the heart.[^e8]

It is tempting to make Harvey the hero of this chapter, and he deserves much of the credit. But notice what his argument depended on: the pulmonary route described by others, the valves described by his teacher, and the habit of checking anatomy against the body rather than the book. Harvey assembled a case from parts, and the decisive part was a number.

## The missing link

There was a hole in the case, and Harvey knew it. If blood leaves the heart in arteries and returns in veins, how does it get from one to the other? Harvey supposed it passed through the tissues somehow, but he never saw it.[^e9]

Marcello Malpighi closed the gap with a microscope. Working on frogs, whose lungs are thin enough to see through, and helped by his colleague Carlo Fracassati, he watched blood moving through vessels too fine for the naked eye, linking the smallest arteries to the smallest veins. The letters describing it were printed in Bologna in 1661.[^e9]

## What the chapter shows

Read in order, the plates in this chapter make a pattern. Each correction came from someone working inside the old system, using its texts and its vocabulary, who found one piece that did not fit. Galen corrected Erasistratus and then built a new error. Ibn al-Nafis corrected Galen inside a commentary on Ibn Sina. Fabricius described what Harvey would reinterpret. Harvey left a gap that Malpighi filled.

That is why this site draws the old beliefs in red ink and scrapes them away gradually rather than all at once. The septal pores fade a little in 1242 and vanish only in 1628. The liver as the source of blood disappears with Harvey. The truth about the circulation was not discovered so much as assembled, over a very long time, by people who were each partly wrong.

A history of anatomy written from Europe tends to treat other traditions in one of two ways. Either they are left out, or they are brought in as early versions of European discoveries: someone in Egypt, India, China or Baghdad who "got there first". This chapter tries to avoid both. It includes the contributions that stand up, it gives one popular claim a hard look, and it points to the Traditions lens for bodies that were mapped by different rules altogether.

## A surgeon's notes from Egypt

The oldest plate on the site is a surgical text. The Edwin Smith papyrus is a copy made around 1700 to 1600 BCE from older material, and it contains the earliest known written descriptions of the surface of the brain, its coverings and the fluid around it.[^e1] Its author was not writing anatomy for its own sake. The papyrus is a set of cases, mostly injuries to the head and upper body, and each one ends with a judgement about what to do. Some are marked as injuries to be treated; some are judged untreatable.[^e2]

That practical frame matters. The papyrus describes the brain because a surgeon looking into a fractured skull needed words for what he saw. Much of the knowledge on this site arrived that way, from the bedside or the battlefield rather than the dissecting table.

## Dissection in a river

The *Suśrutasaṃhitā*, a Sanskrit surgical text, describes a method for studying the body: the body is left in water for several days until it softens, and its layers are then removed one by one.[^e3] The details differ between translations and secondary accounts, and the plate records the disagreement.

The dating is a bigger problem. Popular accounts often place Suśruta in the sixth century BCE. Specialist scholarship treats the text as layered, possibly begun in the last centuries BCE and completed later.[^e4] This site uses the cautious dating. A later date does not make the method less remarkable. It makes the claim about it more defensible.

## A claim that does not survive

The *Huangdi Neijing*, the foundational classic of Chinese medicine, is often said to describe the circulation of the blood centuries before Harvey. That claim depends on a contested translation. Specialist translators read the vessels as carrying qi, and only sometimes blood, and find neither blood circulation nor a pumping heart in the text.[^e5]

The plate for this claim is marked *revised*, and it never inks in. That is not a judgement against Chinese medicine. It is a judgement against a particular way of praising it, which takes a coherent system of its own and grades it on how closely it anticipated a European one. The Traditions lens shows that system on its own terms. Its centrepiece is the Bronze Man: from 1026, by imperial edict, Wang Weiyi oversaw the making of bronze acupuncture figures and an accompanying book, completed around 1027, which served as a national standard for channels and points.[^e6] That is an achievement in standardisation and teaching that needs no comparison with Harvey to be impressive.

## Light entering the eye

Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo in the early eleventh century, wrote the *Book of Optics* between about 1011 and 1021. Against older theories in which the eye sends out rays, he argued that light from every point of an object enters the eye. His account of the eye itself drew on Galenic anatomy.[^e7]

His plate is honest about what that means. Ibn al-Haytham did not discover a new structure. His contribution was to the understanding of vision, and through it to the question of what the eye's parts are for. On the body, the eye fills with colour at his date because the site counts that kind of understanding as knowledge, even when the anatomy was inherited.

## The lung route, three centuries early

Ibn al-Nafis is the strongest case in this chapter. In thirteenth-century Cairo, writing a commentary on the anatomy of Ibn Sina's *Canon*, he rejected the idea that blood passes through pores in the wall of the heart and described it travelling through the lungs instead. The manuscript was forgotten until it was rediscovered in Berlin in 1924 by the Egyptian physician Muhyo al-Deen Altatawi.[^e8]

What happened in between is uncertain. Three Europeans described the same route in the 1550s, and historians disagree about whether Ibn al-Nafis's work could have reached any of them. No documentary chain has been found.[^e9] The plate is marked *contested* for that reason. His description stands either way. What is in doubt is only whether it travelled.

## Knowledge that travelled by translation

Before turning to the lens, it is worth noticing how much of this chapter is about texts moving between languages. Ibn al-Nafis was not working alone or from nothing. He was commenting on Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine*, written in the early eleventh century, which had organised Greek medicine, Galen's anatomy included, into a systematic textbook.[^e12] The *Canon* then made a second journey. Translated into Latin in the twelfth century, traditionally by Gerard of Cremona, it became a standard textbook in European medical schools for centuries.[^e13]

So the line that runs from Galen to Harvey does not run through Europe alone. Greek anatomy was preserved, organised, argued with and extended in Arabic, and it reached the universities of Bologna, Padua and Paris partly in translations from Arabic. When a Renaissance student learned that blood seeps through the septum, they were often learning Galen through Ibn Sina, in Latin. When Ibn al-Nafis rejected that idea, he was arguing inside the same tradition.

That is a better way to see the Islamic contributions on this site than as a rival to Europe. Galen's medicine was not simply stored in Arabic until Europe was ready for it; it was taught, organised and disputed there, and the European anatomists of the sixteenth century inherited that work along with the Greek texts behind it. The fog on this site shows *when* each piece of knowledge arrived. This section is a reminder that *where* it arrived from is often a longer story than the plate has room for.

## Bodies drawn by other rules

The Traditions lens adds three more maps of the body, and each one is worth reading as a different answer to the question "what is a body made of?"

In late fourteenth- or early fifteenth-century Persia, Mansur ibn Ilyas wrote an anatomy dedicated to Pir Muhammad Bahadur, ruler of Fars. It is the first known Islamic anatomical text with full-body illustrations, and it is organised into five systems, each with its own figure: bones, nerves, muscles, veins and arteries.[^e10] The anatomy is largely Galenic. The way it is laid out, system by system, would look familiar to a modern student.

The Ayurvedic Man in the Wellcome Collection is an eighteenth-century painting, probably made in Kathmandu by an Ayurvedic physician, Tibetan artists and a calligrapher. It shows the organs of the torso as receptacles for the body's fluids, captioned with passages from a sixteenth- or seventeenth-century Sanskrit text.[^e11] It is a map of processes, not of shapes.

The Bronze Man, already described, maps channels and points: places to act on the body, not parts to be named.

None of these is a wrong version of a modern atlas. Each answers a question the modern atlas does not ask. That is why the lens shows them beside the figure, linked to the nearest modern structure where a link makes sense and labelled clearly where it does not, instead of folding them into the fog.

## What this chapter does and does not claim

It claims that knowledge about the body was produced in many places and passed between them, often through translation. It does not claim that every tradition was secretly working towards modern anatomy, and it does not rank traditions by how early they reached a modern answer. Where a contribution can be checked against sources, the site checks it, and where a popular claim fails that check, as the *Huangdi Neijing* claim does, it says so. Accuracy is a form of respect too.

There is a practical reason for this caution as well. Claims about who got there first are the claims most likely to be shared, and most likely to be repeated without their sources. A site that wants to be trusted on the contributions that are real has to be just as careful about the ones that are not. The Ibn al-Nafis plate is stronger, not weaker, for sitting beside a *Huangdi Neijing* plate that is marked revised. Both are judged by the same rules, and both show their sources.

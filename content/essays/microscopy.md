Every plate in this chapter turns on an instrument or a technique: a lens, a stain, a new way of preparing tissue. And almost every plate carries the same lesson, which is the reason the fog on this site treats microscopic observation as *described* rather than *understood*. To see something is not yet to know what it is for.

## A closed circuit

The chapter opens where the circulation chapter closes. Harvey's argument of 1628 needed a connection between the smallest arteries and the smallest veins, and he could not see one. In 1661 Marcello Malpighi, writing two letters from Bologna, used a microscope to show first that the lung is not solid flesh but a mass of tiny air sacs, and then, working on the thin lungs of frogs with Carlo Fracassati, watched blood moving through vessels too fine for the naked eye.[^e1]

This was a case where seeing and understanding arrived together, because the understanding was already waiting. Harvey had predicted the vessels. Malpighi found them. Most of the other plates in this chapter are less tidy.

## The animalcules

In August 1677 a medical student, Johan Ham, brought Antonie van Leeuwenhoek a sample of semen in which he had seen tiny moving creatures. Leeuwenhoek, a Delft cloth merchant who ground his own extraordinary single lenses, looked and saw them too. That November he reported them to the Royal Society in London, and recorded that Ham had seen them first.[^e2]

The credit is the lesson here. Popular history usually says Leeuwenhoek discovered sperm, and Leeuwenhoek himself said otherwise. But there is a second lesson underneath it. Seeing the cells did not explain conception. That would take a great deal longer, and on this site the testes ink in as an outline in 1677, not in colour.

## Tissues without a lens

The most surprising plate in this chapter has no microscope in it at all. In 1800 the Parisian anatomist Xavier Bichat argued in his *Traité des membranes* that the body's organs are built from a limited set of tissues, combined in different ways, and he distinguished twenty-one of them. He did it by looking at and handling tissue with the naked eye.[^e3]

Bichat belongs here because he changed the *level* at which anatomy looked. Before him, the natural unit was the organ. After him, it was the tissue. The microscope would soon push the level down again, to the cell, but the habit of asking what organs are made of began with a man who did not use one.

## Cells, and where they come from

In 1838 and 1839 Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann proposed that plants and animals alike are built from cells. It was a unifying idea, and it contained a mistake: Schwann thought new cells crystallised out of a formless fluid. The mistake was corrected by Robert Remak's observations of cells dividing and by Rudolf Virchow's principle that every cell comes from a cell.[^e4] The dates and the credit are argued over; Remak, in particular, is often thought to have been underacknowledged. The note on the source records the disagreement.

Cell theory is not one of the twenty-six plates, because it is not about one place on the body. But it is the frame in which the remaining plates of this chapter make sense.

## Seen in 1869, understood in 1921

In 1869 Paul Langerhans, a medical student in Berlin, described small, distinct clusters of cells scattered through the pancreas. They were later named the islets of Langerhans, and their role in producing insulin was established only in the twentieth century.[^e5]

That role was found in Toronto in 1921 and 1922. Frederick Banting and Charles Best, working in J. J. R. Macleod's department, prepared a pancreatic extract that lowered blood sugar, and James Collip purified it for use in patients.[^e6] The credit for this was fought over bitterly, and the plate for it is marked contested for that reason alone.

If you want one demonstration of why this site draws knowledge in two stages, drag the slider across these years. The pancreas inks in as an outline in 1869 and fills with colour only in 1921. Half a century separates seeing the islets from understanding them.

## A stain that answered back

The nervous system was the hardest tissue to see clearly, because its cells are tangled together. Camillo Golgi's silver stain solved that by blackening only a few cells, whole, against a pale background. Santiago Ramón y Cajal improved the method and used it, in 1888 and 1889, to show that nerve cells are separate units rather than a continuous net.[^e7] The neurons chapter tells that story. What matters here is the instrument: a chemical trick that made a structure visible, which then settled an argument about function.

## Molecules in motion

The last plate in this chapter reaches below the cell. On 22 May 1954, *Nature* published two papers side by side. Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke had used interference microscopy on living muscle fibres; Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson had studied isolated myofibrils. Working independently, they reached the same conclusion: during contraction the filaments in muscle keep their length and slide past each other.[^e8]

Muscle had been described for millennia. On this site it inks in as an outline from Suśruta's text onwards and fills with colour only in 1954, because only then was its working explained.

## How to read these plates

There is a temptation, looking back, to treat each new instrument as a window that simply opened. The plates in this chapter suggest a different picture. An instrument produces an image, and an image has to be interpreted by someone who already holds ideas about what they are looking at. Malpighi could read his frog lung because Harvey had told him what to expect.[^e1] Schwann looked at real cells and still wrote a wrong account of where they came from.[^e4] Golgi and Cajal looked at preparations made with the same stain and argued for opposite conclusions.[^e7]

That is why this site separates two moments for each structure. The first is when someone could reliably describe it: its shape, its place, what it is made of. The second is when someone could say what it does, and back that up with an experiment or an argument others could check. For the gross anatomy in earlier chapters, the two moments are often close together. At the scale of cells they drift apart, sometimes by decades.

It also explains why so many plates in this chapter carry a note about credit. A finding under the microscope is easy to repeat once someone knows what to look for, and hard to see beforehand. A student shows a merchant something in a drop of fluid.[^e2] Two teams publish the same conclusion in the same issue of a journal.[^e8] A purified extract needs a chemist as much as a surgeon.[^e6] The question 'who saw it first?' is usually less interesting than the question 'what did they need to know in order to see it?'

## What the chapter shows

Each instrument in this chapter widened what could be described. Not one of them, on its own, delivered understanding. Malpighi's lens confirmed a prediction. Leeuwenhoek's showed something nobody could yet explain. Bichat saw tissues without a lens at all. Langerhans saw islets half a century before anyone knew what they did. Golgi's stain was turned against Golgi's own theory.

The gap between seeing and understanding is the gap the fog on this site is built to show. Microscopes made the body visible at new scales. Experiments, arguments and sometimes decades of waiting made it intelligible.

That is also why the colour on this site arrives later than the outlines in this chapter. It is tempting to think of an outline as an unfinished drawing, waiting for someone to fill it in. In the history of the microscope it was usually something more interesting: an honest record that people had seen a thing clearly and did not yet know what it was.
